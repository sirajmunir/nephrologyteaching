Assessing the renal patient

History, bloods, volume status, urinalysis and when to pick up the phone. Work through the six steps before the session; each one has a short read and something to try. Allow about 45 minutes.

By the end you should be able to

  1. Take a focused history that sorts a creatinine rise into pre-renal, intrinsic and post-renal causes, and audit the drug chart.
  2. Stage AKI with KDIGO/NICE criteria and classify CKD by eGFR (G) and albumin:creatinine ratio (A).
  3. Assess volume status at the bedside without relying on any single sign.
  4. Read a urine dipstick, know its blind spots, and know when to send ACR, PCR or microscopy.
  5. Decide who needs a same-day renal call, urology, or a routine outpatient referral, and hand over in a way the renal registrar can act on.

Step 1 of 6

The history: three questions before anything else

A raised creatinine is a finding, not a diagnosis. Your history should answer three things: is this acute, chronic, or acute-on-chronic? Where is the problem (before, in, or after the kidney)? And what in the drug chart is making it worse?

1 · Find the baseline first

Before you take the history, find previous creatinine results: hospital lab system, GP record or Summary Care Record, old discharge letters. Without a baseline you cannot stage AKI or tell it apart from CKD.

Clues that a raised creatinine is chronic Previous abnormal results, normocytic anaemia, raised phosphate and PTH, small kidneys (<9 cm) or thinned cortex on ultrasound, and long-standing diabetes or hypertension. Large kidneys with CKD: think polycystic kidney disease, diabetes, amyloid, or myeloma.

2 · Localise the problem

Pre-renal: poor perfusionThe commonest cause of AKI on the wards
  • Fluid losses: vomiting, diarrhoea, stoma output, bleeding, burns, over-diuresis.
  • Poor intake: confusion, dysphagia, nil by mouth, frailty.
  • Low effective circulating volume: sepsis, heart failure, cirrhosis, nephrotic syndrome.
  • Haemodynamic drugs: ACE inhibitors, ARBs, diuretics, NSAIDs, SGLT2 inhibitors, antihypertensives in a hypotensive patient.

Prolonged pre-renal injury becomes acute tubular injury. "Pre-renal" is a reason to act early, not a reason to relax.

Intrinsic: damage to the kidney itselfThe causes you must not miss
  • Glomerulonephritis / vasculitis: rash, arthralgia, haemoptysis, sinusitis, epistaxis, hearing change, weight loss, night sweats. Visible haematuria 1–2 days after a sore throat suggests IgA nephropathy; 1–3 weeks after suggests post-infectious GN.
  • Acute interstitial nephritis: new drug in the past weeks to months (PPIs, antibiotics, NSAIDs). Rash, fever and eosinophilia are often absent.
  • Myeloma: bone pain, anaemia, hypercalcaemia, recurrent infection, older patient with AKI and no clear cause.
  • Rhabdomyolysis: falls or long lie, crush injury, seizures, statin plus interacting drug, extreme exercise, recreational drugs.
  • Nephrotoxins: aminoglycosides, vancomycin, iodinated contrast, lithium, calcineurin inhibitors, chemotherapy.
  • Thrombotic microangiopathy: bloody diarrhoea (HUS), new thrombocytopenia, anaemia with fragments.
Post-renal: obstructionReversible, so always consider it
  • Lower urinary tract symptoms, prostatism, previous retention, palpable bladder.
  • Pelvic or abdominal malignancy, retroperitoneal fibrosis, renal stones, loin pain.
  • Drugs that cause retention: anticholinergics, opioids.
  • Single functioning kidney: obstruction of that one kidney causes AKI.
  • Anuria (no urine at all) is obstruction or a blocked catheter until proven otherwise; flush or change the catheter and scan the bladder.
Background, family and social historyContext that changes the plan
  • Risk factors listed in NICE NG148: CKD, previous AKI, diabetes, heart failure, liver disease, age 65 and over, sepsis, hypovolaemia, nephrotoxic drugs, cognitive impairment or dependence on others for fluids, contrast, urological obstruction.
  • Known kidney disease: CKD stage, renal clinic follow-up, dialysis, kidney transplant (call renal early).
  • Family history: polycystic kidney disease, Alport syndrome (with deafness), other inherited disease.
  • Pregnancy or recent delivery: pre-eclampsia, sepsis, haemorrhage.
  • Recreational drugs: cocaine (rhabdomyolysis, vasculitis with levamisole), ketamine (bladder and ureteric damage).
  • Frailty, baseline function and patient wishes: these shape ceilings of care, including whether dialysis is appropriate.
Symptoms of advanced kidney diseaseUraemia and its complications

Fatigue, nausea, anorexia, pruritus, cramps, restless legs, breathlessness, ankle swelling, frothy urine (proteinuria), nocturia, confusion. Pleuritic chest pain with a rub can mean uraemic pericarditis, which is an indication for urgent dialysis.

3 · Audit the drug chart

Mrs D, 76, admitted with three days of diarrhoea. Creatinine 198 µmol/L (baseline 88 three months ago). BP 96/58. Decide for each drug on her chart whether to hold or review while she has AKI, or continue. Then check your answers.

Sick day rules: "SADMANS" Sulfonylureas, ACE inhibitors, Diuretics, Metformin, ARBs, NSAIDs, SGLT2 inhibitors. Pause during acute dehydrating illness and restart within 24–48 hours of eating and drinking normally. Every discharge summary after AKI should say what was stopped and when to restart it.
Creatinine can rise without any fall in GFR Trimethoprim and cimetidine block tubular creatinine secretion; a large meat meal and creatine supplements add to the creatinine load. Low muscle mass (frail, older, amputee) does the opposite and hides poor function behind a "normal" creatinine.
Step 2 of 6

Labs: creatinine, eGFR, staging, and what else to send

Creatinine lags behind injury and depends on muscle mass. eGFR is only valid in a steady state. Know what each number can and cannot tell you.

eGFR in UK practice

  • UK labs report eGFR using the CKD-EPI creatinine equation, and NICE NG203 advises no adjustment for ethnicity.
  • Not valid in AKI: creatinine has not reached steady state, so eGFR overestimates function while creatinine is still rising.
  • Also unreliable at extremes of muscle mass, in amputees, in pregnancy and in under-18s.
  • NICE suggests eGFR from cystatin C to confirm or rule out CKD when eGFRcreatinine is 45–59 for 90 days or more with no proteinuria or other marker of kidney disease.
  • For drug dosing, check the BNF or SPC; many use creatinine clearance (Cockcroft–Gault) rather than eGFR, and this matters for narrow-margin drugs such as DOACs and aminoglycosides.

Defining AKI NICE NG148 KDIGO 2012

Any one of:

  • Creatinine rise of ≥26 µmol/L within 48 hours
  • Creatinine rise of ≥50% (×1.5) within the past 7 days
  • Urine output <0.5 ml/kg/h for more than 6 hours

NHS lab systems flag "AKI warning stage" results using the NHS England algorithm, which compares the current result with the lowest in the last 7 days or the median of the last year.

Classifying CKD: the G and A grid KDIGO 2024 NICE NG203

CKD means abnormal kidney structure or function present for more than 3 months: eGFR <60, or ACR ≥3 mg/mmol, or other markers such as haematuria of renal origin or structural abnormality. Always report both letters, for example "CKD G3b A2". Tap a cell.

The rest of the bloods

On every patient with AKI or advanced CKD
  • Potassium. UK Kidney Association grading: mild 5.5–5.9, moderate 6.0–6.4, severe ≥6.5 mmol/L. Severe hyperkalaemia or any ECG change is an emergency; follow your trust's hyperkalaemia algorithm.
  • Venous gas: pH and bicarbonate. Metabolic acidosis is common; severe or worsening acidosis despite treatment is an indication to discuss dialysis.
  • Urea. Rises out of proportion to creatinine with dehydration, upper GI bleeding, steroids and catabolism. Use it as context, not as a measure of GFR.
  • Full blood count. Anaemia points towards chronicity or myeloma; low platelets with anaemia raise TMA; neutrophilia suggests sepsis; eosinophilia raises interstitial nephritis or cholesterol emboli.
  • Calcium, phosphate, albumin, CRP. High calcium suggests myeloma or sarcoid; very high phosphate suggests tumour lysis, rhabdomyolysis or chronic disease.
  • CK if there is any reason to think of rhabdomyolysis.
AKI with no obvious cause: the "renal screen"Send before you call renal; it is the first thing they will ask for
  • Urine dipstick (before a catheter if possible) and urine ACR or PCR.
  • ANCA (MPO and PR3), anti-GBM antibodies, ANA, complement C3 and C4.
  • Serum free light chains and serum protein electrophoresis; immunoglobulins.
  • Hepatitis B, hepatitis C and HIV serology where relevant, and before any immunosuppression.
  • If TMA is possible: blood film for fragments, LDH, haptoglobin, reticulocytes, and stool culture if there is bloody diarrhoea.
  • Renal tract ultrasound (see referral step for timing).
Step 3 of 6

Volume status: no single sign will tell you

You are really asking two questions. Is the circulation under-filled, and would more fluid help? Is there too much fluid in the wrong place? A patient can be oedematous and intravascularly depleted at the same time.

Build the picture from

  • Trends: heart rate, blood pressure (and postural BP if safe), NEWS2, daily weights, fluid balance, urine output.
  • Perfusion: capillary refill, peripheral temperature, mental state, lactate.
  • Venous pressure: JVP at 45°, hepatojugular reflux.
  • Extravascular fluid: lung crackles, pleural effusions, sacral and leg oedema, ascites.
  • Response to a challenge: passive leg raise, or a fluid bolus with reassessment.
  • Bedside ultrasound (lung B-lines, IVC) is useful in trained hands; interpret with the rest of the picture.

IV fluids: the 5 Rs NICE CG174

  • Resuscitation: if the patient needs it, give a 500 ml bolus of crystalloid with sodium 130–154 mmol/L over less than 15 minutes, then reassess.
  • Routine maintenance: 25–30 ml/kg/day water, about 1 mmol/kg/day each of sodium, potassium and chloride, and 50–100 g/day glucose. Consider less (20–25 ml/kg/day) in older or frail patients, heart failure and kidney impairment.
  • Replacement of ongoing losses, matched to what is being lost.
  • Redistribution: oedema, sepsis, hypoalbuminaemia, post-op.
  • Reassessment: every fluid prescription needs a plan for review.

Step 4 of 6

Urinalysis: the cheapest renal investigation

NICE NG148 asks for a urine dipstick in everyone with suspected or confirmed AKI. Dip it early, ideally before a catheter goes in, because catheter trauma causes haematuria and muddies the picture.

Explore the dipstick

Tap each pad for what it detects, its pitfalls, and what it means in kidney patients.

Blood and protein on the dipstick + AKI, without a clear urinary infection Think glomerulonephritis or vasculitis. Send the renal screen and urine ACR or PCR, check the blood pressure, and discuss with renal the same day. Rapidly progressive GN can destroy kidney function within days.

Quantifying protein

  • Urine ACR (albumin:creatinine ratio) is the test NICE recommends for detecting and monitoring CKD. A dipstick is not good enough for this.
  • ACR categories: A1 <3, A2 3–30, A3 >30 mg/mmol. An ACR of 3 or more is clinically significant; confirm a first result between 3 and 70 on an early-morning sample.
  • Urine PCR (protein:creatinine ratio) measures total protein. It is used in many renal units to monitor known glomerular disease.
  • A dipstick and ACR mainly detect albumin. They miss light chains, so if you suspect myeloma send serum free light chains and protein electrophoresis instead.
  • Nephrotic syndrome: heavy proteinuria, hypoalbuminaemia and oedema. It needs prompt renal discussion and thought about VTE risk.

Haematuria in practice UKKA

  • Significant haematuria is 1+ or more on dipstick. Trace is not significant. Microscopy is not needed to confirm a positive dipstick.
  • Exclude transient causes first: UTI, menstruation, vigorous exercise, recent catheterisation.
  • Persistent non-visible haematuria means 2 of 3 dipsticks positive.
  • For all persistent haematuria: check BP, eGFR and urine ACR. These decide between urology and nephrology.
  • Urology, suspected cancer pathway NG12: age 45 and over with unexplained visible haematuria (no UTI, or persisting after UTI treated); age 60 and over with unexplained non-visible haematuria plus dysuria or a raised white cell count.
  • Nephrology: haematuria with ACR ≥30, falling eGFR, or hypertension in a younger patient; visible haematuria during an upper respiratory infection.

Step 5 of 6

Referral: who, how fast, and what to say

Most AKI is managed by the admitting team. The skill is spotting the patients who need a renal or urology opinion today, and making the call useful.

Imaging the renal tract NG148

  • Do not routinely scan when a cause of AKI has been found.
  • Scan within 24 hours if there is no identified cause, or if obstruction is suspected.
  • Scan within 6 hours if pyonephrosis (infected, obstructed kidney) is suspected.
  • Urology immediately for pyonephrosis, an obstructed solitary kidney, bilateral upper tract obstruction, or complications of obstruction. Nephrostomy or stenting should happen as soon as possible and within 12 hours of diagnosis.

Indications to discuss dialysis now

  • Hyperkalaemia not responding to medical treatment.
  • Metabolic acidosis that is severe or worsening.
  • Fluid overload or pulmonary oedema not responding to diuretics.
  • Uraemic complications: pericarditis, encephalopathy, bleeding.
  • Dialysable poisons (for example lithium, metformin, ethylene glycol).

Involve critical care as well as renal for an unstable patient. Agree and document ceilings of care early.

One sentence first "I'm calling about a 34-year-old with AKI, creatinine 310 with no baseline, blood 3+ and protein 3+ on dipstick and a BP of 170/100. I'm worried about a glomerulonephritis and would like your advice today." Then give the detail.

CKD referral from clinic or the ward NICE NG203

Refer adults with CKD for specialist assessment if they have any of:

  • A 5-year risk of needing renal replacement therapy greater than 5%, using the 4-variable Kidney Failure Risk Equation (age, sex, eGFR, ACR). Use a validated calculator such as kidneyfailurerisk.com.
  • ACR of 70 mg/mmol or more, unless known to be caused by diabetes and already appropriately treated.
  • ACR of 30 mg/mmol or more (A3) together with haematuria.
  • A sustained fall in eGFR of 25% or more with a change in eGFR category within 12 months.
  • A sustained fall in eGFR of 15 ml/min/1.73 m² or more per year.
  • Hypertension poorly controlled despite at least 4 antihypertensives at therapeutic doses.
  • Known or suspected rare or genetic causes of CKD, or suspected renal artery stenosis.

eGFR below 30 (G4–G5) usually warrants referral too, taking the person's wishes and comorbidities into account.

Key messages to take to the session

  1. Find the baseline before you label anything as AKI or CKD.
  2. Sort every creatinine rise into pre-renal, intrinsic and post-renal, and audit the drug chart on day one.
  3. eGFR is meaningless while creatinine is changing; stage AKI with creatinine and urine output.
  4. Volume status is a synthesis of trends and signs. Reassess after every intervention.
  5. Dip the urine early. Blood plus protein with AKI is a same-day renal conversation.
  6. Call with a one-line summary, the baseline, the trend, the dipstick and the potassium.
